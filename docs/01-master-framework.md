## 1. Setting

The Cassena Islands sit at about 30° N off the southern end of a mainland mountain range, at the corner where the Atlantic coast meets a large Gulf-side bay. They are two very different things joined by a narrow inland sea:

- **The highland islands** (Halcomb and Graystone) are the drowned southern tip of the mainland range — old crystalline rock, high enough at 2,047 m to carry spruce–fir forest even at this latitude.
- **The coastal-plain islands** (Bellamy, Saint Ambrose, Ossahatchee, Wickham) and their fringing barriers and keys are young sediment: sand, clay, marl and limestone laid down by the sea and by rivers flowing off the highlands.
- **Cassena Sound** separates them: a rift trough, 14 m deep with a scoured channel to 55 m, running east–west along a fault line. Its straight northern shore is the fault scarp of the highlands. On its two small central islands sit the city of Calder and the industrial port of Corliss.

This arrangement is the single idea that the rest of the world follows. Moving south across the islands is moving down the geological staircase of the American Southeast: mountains → fall line → upper coastal plain → lower coastal plain → barrier islands and subtropical keys.

### Island specialisation

No two islands share a dominant environment, and each identity is a consequence of geology and position rather than a theme:

| Island | Why it is this kind of island |
|---|---|
| **Halcomb** — Appalachian highland | The highest block of the crystalline range: a 2,000 m crest, deep coves, spruce–fir summits, a limestone valley and a sandstone tableland on its western flank, mirroring the real sequence Blue Ridge → Valley and Ridge → plateau. |
| **Graystone** — escarpment island | A tilted plateau of granite and gneiss: a gentle, cool highland surface to the north and a 900 m south-facing escarpment (the Blue Wall) that wrings rain out of every southerly wind; the wettest, most waterfall-rich place in the islands. |
| **Calder** — dense urban | A bedrock knob at the mouth of the largest river, beside the deepest water in the Sound and at the head of ocean navigation: every reason for a city in one place, on an island too small to sprawl, so it grew up. |
| **Corliss** — industrial | A flat island of delta mud and dredge spoil beside the ship channel: deep water, flat land, rail and no neighbours — exactly where a port, refinery, paper mill and power plant go. |
| **Bellamy** — agricultural | The upper coastal plain: well-drained sandy loams, red clay hills, Carolina-bay lakes and a gentle river — the best farmland in the islands. |
| **Mirabel** — tourist coast | A Gulf-facing barrier of white quartz sand with low wave energy, warm water and sunsets over the bay. |
| **Saint Ambrose** — Lowcountry | Pleistocene sea-island cores ringed by vast tidal marsh, with deep tidal rivers, live-oak avenues and an Atlantic barrier beach. |
| **Wickham** — wilderness | Limestone close to the surface under a sand ridge: springs, sinks and dry scrub that never repaid farming, so it was never settled. |
| **Ossahatchee** — swamp | The lowest, flattest and wettest land in the islands, held up by one relict beach ridge on its eastern edge. |
| **The Gannet Banks** — barrier chain | Thin sand islands in front of a shallow lagoon, fully exposed to the Atlantic: capes, inlets, shoals and the largest dunes on the coast. |
| **The Sabal Keys** — mangrove keys | The southernmost, warmest land: low limestone keys with mangrove at its northern limit. |

## 3. Positions and distances

The islands form two tiers and a fringe:

- **North tier** — Halcomb and Graystone, separated by Hollow Reach (a drowned gorge 0.75–2.8 km wide and 48 m deep). Halcomb is 0.6 km from the mainland across The Narrows at its narrowest point.
- **Central water** — Cassena Sound, 9–15 km wide, with Calder and Corliss in its middle.
- **South tier** — Bellamy and Saint Ambrose (divided by the tidal Ashwood River, 0.3 km at its narrowest), Wickham and Ossahatchee (divided by Sawpit Pass, 0.4 km), with Ketch Sound (a tidal strait) between the two rows.
- **Fringe** — Mirabel on the Gulf-bay side (0.4 km across Mirabel Sound from Bellamy), the Gannet Banks on the Atlantic side (enclosing Tarrow Sound, 5 km off Ossahatchee), and the Sabal Keys to the south (1.4 km off Ossahatchee).

| **Graystone Highlands** | Graystone | Whitlock Mountain, 1,724 m | A tilted granite and gneiss plateau, 960–1,080 m, topped by the Whitlock Range; bounded on the south by the Blue Wall escarpment and on the east by the Fallon Cliffs (210 m sea cliffs). |

## 6. Watersheds and rivers

The archipelago has three drainage styles:

1. **Highland rivers** (Halcomb, Graystone): steep, rocky, fast, with falls where they leave crystalline rock (the fall line along Halcomb's south foot and the foot of the Blue Wall). Mean gradients of 37–200 m/km in the headwaters.
2. **Coastal-plain rivers** (Bellamy, Ossahatchee): slow, meandering, tannin-dark, with oxbows and wide floodplains; 1–5 m/km.
3. **Tidal rivers and spring runs** (Saint Ambrose, Wickham): the Lowcountry rivers are tidal throughout, drowned valleys in the marsh; Wickham's rivers are clear spring runs from the limestone aquifer.

|---|---|---|---|---|

## 7. Climate and prevailing weather

- **Summer (May–September):** The subtropical high sits to the east and pumps warm, humid air from the south-east. Sea breezes from the Gulf and Atlantic collide over the flat southern islands and build afternoon thunderstorms almost daily; over the highlands, the same moist flow is lifted against the Blue Wall and the Balsam Crest's southern slopes and rains out. The Blue Wall's south face is the wettest place in the islands (about 2,500 mm a year); the Coldwater Valley, sheltered behind the crest, is noticeably drier (about 1,200 mm).
- **Autumn (September–November):** The tropical-storm season peaks in September. Hurricanes approach most often from the south-east across the Atlantic (striking the Gannet Banks and Ambrose Beach first) and less often from the Gulf (striking Mirabel and Wickham). The Banks' overwash fans, the eroding Ambrose shore and the storm-pruned maritime forest are the physical record of this. October brings the clearest air of the year and colour on the highlands.
- **Winter (December–February):** Cold fronts sweep in from the north-west across the mainland every few days. Behind them come clear, dry, very visible days — the days when the Ledford Dome tower can be seen from Calder. Above about 1,400 m, rime ice and snow are normal; the Balsam Crest Parkway closes after ice storms. Nor'easters bring cold rain and high surf to the Atlantic side.
- **Spring (March–April):** The shortest, most changeable season; dogwood and redbud bloom low in March and climb the mountains through May.
- **Fog:** radiation fog in the valleys and over the rivers on calm nights (Coldwater Valley, Tennalee Gorge, Ocosta bottoms); advection sea fog over The Narrows and the cold Atlantic shelf in spring; marsh fog on still mornings in the Lowcountry and the swamp.

## 8. Ocean exposure

| Coast | Exposure | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Atlantic (east) — Gannet Banks, Ambrose Beach | High-energy swell, hurricane and nor'easter landfall | Wide beaches, big dunes, inlets, shoals, erosion, overwash. |
| Gulf / Serena Bay (west) — Mirabel, Wickham's west coast | Low energy, warm, shallow shelf | Fine white beaches; marsh and oyster-bar shores where sand supply is low. |
| Cassena Sound (centre) | Sheltered, tidal | Marsh fringes, bluffs and harbours; the city and port. |
| The Narrows (north) | Strait with strong tidal current | Cliffed coast under the North Face; cold water and fog. |
| Southern flats — around the Sabal Keys | Very shallow, sheltered | Seagrass, mangrove, mud and shell. |

## 9. Relationship to the mainland

The mainland lies north of The Narrows (outside the playable frame). It supplies the three lifelines: I-21 and the Mainland Main Line railway cross The Narrows on the Narrows Bridge and the Narrows Rail Bridge, and the 500 kV transmission line crosses on 150 m towers. The archipelago is therefore entered from the north through the mountains, and the journey south down I-21 is the journey down the geological staircase.

## 10. Major transport connections

- **I-21** — from the mainland over the Narrows Bridge, south along the Coldwater Valley, through Shady Gap, east along Halcomb's south coast, over the Tennalee River Bridge to Calder, then over the 3.5 km Long Bridge to Bellamy Bluffs and Calder International Airport.
- **I-121** — the port spur from Calder over the Harbor Bridge to the Corliss terminals.
- **SR 17 Coastal Highway** — from Corliss over the South Sound Bridge to Saint Ambrose and Haversham, south across the Ketch Sound Bridge to Ossahatchee, then down Long Ridge to Sabal Landing; **SR 1 Keys Highway** continues across the Sabal Bridges.
- **SR 29 The Trail** — the only road across the swamp, from Ossahatchee west along Long Ridge and the Trail causeway to the Sawpit Pass Bridge and Wickham.
- **SR 40 Cross-Island Highway** — Mirabel Causeway → Bellamy → Ashwood River Bridge → Haversham.
- **SR 14 Banks Highway** — Haversham → Ambrose Beach → Ambrose Inlet Bridge → the Gannet Banks → Merrin, with a free ferry over Rockfish Inlet to Pennick.
- **Gate Road (SR 26)** — Halcomb to Graystone by the Bearpen Tunnel and the 249 m-high Gate Bridge across Hollow Reach.
- **Ferries** — Graystone–Calder (vehicle ferry, 16 kn), Tarrow Sound Ferry (Tarrow Landing to the Banks), Rockfish Inlet free ferry, Wickham cable ferry, and a seasonal fast ferry from Calder to Mirabel.
- **Railways** — the Mainland Main Line (to Calder), the Corliss Freight Line, the Bellamy & Southern (Corliss → Saint Ambrose → Bellamy → Wickham ferry), the Haversham Branch and the Ocosta nuclear spur.
- **Air** — Calder International on the Bellamy Bluffs, with regional airports at Haversham, Mirabel–Bellamy and Hickory Tableland, and airstrips at Merrin, Wickham and Ocosta.

Each connection is described in detail, with lengths and engineering, in the island profiles and [13 — World Scale](13-world-scale.md).
